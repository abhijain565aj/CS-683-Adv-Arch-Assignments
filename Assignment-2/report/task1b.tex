\section{Task 1B: Stride Pattern Prefetcher (SPP)}
\label{sec:task1b}

\subsection{Data structures and operation}

The implemented \texttt{spp.l1d\_pref} uses three tracking structures: a 24-entry IP tracker with LRU replacement, a 1,024-entry directly indexed signature table, and a 512-entry prefetch tracking table. The IP key is masked to its lower eight bits. Each IP tracker stores an IP tag, the last cache-line address and stride, an LRU rank, four recent strides, a history count, a confidence counter, and a valid flag. On an IP miss, an invalid tracker is preferred; if all trackers are occupied, the least recently used entry is replaced and its history is reset.

For a tracked IP, the observed stride is the difference between the current cache-line address and the address of its preceding access. The corrected implementation updates \texttt{last\_cl\_addr} immediately after computing this difference, including when the stride is zero. This is necessary for consecutive strides such as $+2,+2,+2$ to be recognized as uniform, rather than measuring every stride from the tracker's first address. Zero strides do not train the signature predictor.

For each nonzero stride, the prefetcher updates a four-stride history. Once four previous strides are available, it forms a 64-bit signature using $s_{i+1}=31s_i+h_i$ over the history values and indexes the signature table using \texttt{signature \% 1024}. Each signature entry stores a full signature tag, the next observed stride, a confidence counter, and a valid flag. A matching prediction increments confidence up to 7; a mismatch replaces the predicted stride and resets confidence to 1. A signature prediction is used only when its tag matches and confidence is at least 2.

Uniform prediction takes priority. The per-IP confidence is increased when two successive nonzero strides match and reset when they differ. If this confidence is at least 1, the prefetcher predicts the current stride; otherwise, it attempts signature-based prediction using the recent history. This provides a simple transition from uniform to non-uniform prediction when the constant-stride pattern breaks. The prefetcher generates up to the selected degree of requests, updating a temporary history after each predicted stride. It stops if a candidate address is negative or falls outside the current OS page. Requests target L1D when MSHR occupancy is below half its capacity and otherwise target L2.

\subsection{Accuracy-based degree control}

The implementation maintains \texttt{issued} and \texttt{useful} counters for adaptive degree control. An accepted prefetch is recorded in a 512-entry address-indexed table with state 1 (issued). A matching L1D prefetch fill changes the state to 2; a subsequent demand hit on that line increments \texttt{useful} and clears the entry. The internal accuracy estimate uses the changes in these counters since the previous update. It is therefore a tracked L1D-prefetch usefulness estimate, and is not necessarily identical to ChampSim's reported prefetch accuracy, especially for requests targeting L2.

After at least 256 additional accepted prefetch requests, the degree is adjusted using the measured usefulness ratio. The prefetcher starts with degree 3 and follows the policy below.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Accuracy-based degree selection in SPP.}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.45\linewidth}p{0.10\linewidth}p{0.37\linewidth}}
\toprule Accuracy since previous update & Degree & Rationale \\
\midrule
Below 30\% & 1 & Reduce traffic when many predictions are not useful \\
30\% to below 60\% & 2 & Balance lookahead and possible cache pollution \\
60\% or above & 3 & Use more lookahead when predictions are frequently useful \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 30\% and 60\% thresholds are simple heuristic cutoffs, not values selected through an independent parameter sweep. Degrees 1, 2, and 3 provide three levels of aggressiveness without additional predictor structures. An update interval of 256 accepted requests avoids changing the degree after every individual prefetch. Larger degrees can improve coverage for predictable access sequences but may waste bandwidth and evict useful lines when predictions are inaccurate.

The exact in-memory size depends on the compiler and can be checked using \texttt{sizeof}.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The corrected SPP improves IPC over the no-prefetch baseline on all three traces, by approximately 0.38\%, 0.02\%, and 0.17\% on Tr1, Tr2, and Tr3. IP-stride has slightly higher IPC improvements of approximately 0.48\%, 0.04\%, and 0.21\%, respectively. The differences are small, but IP-stride remains the best of the three evaluated configurations in IPC on each trace.

SPP achieves higher reported prefetch accuracy than IP-stride on every trace: 56.4\% versus 52.7\% on Tr1, 51.8\% versus 38.2\% on Tr2, and 18.6\% versus 16.0\% on Tr3. However, IP-stride maintains higher L1D coverage (5.2\%, 0.74\%, and 2.5\%) than SPP (3.7\%, 0.61\%, and 1.4\%). The corresponding L1D load MPKI values also remain slightly lower for IP-stride. Thus, better accuracy for SPP does not translate into greater miss coverage or higher IPC in these experiments.

These results suggest that the corrected SPP makes more selective prefetches but does not hide as many demand misses as IP-stride on the three tested traces. Possible contributing factors include limited signature-table capacity, masked IP aliasing, prefetch-degree throttling, and fallback behavior. The measurements do not isolate the effects of these design choices, and they are not enough to conclude that non-uniform stride prediction is generally less effective. The results are specific to the implemented policy and the supplied workloads.
